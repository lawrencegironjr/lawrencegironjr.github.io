Thesis

Characterizing Environmental Severity and Atmospheric Corrosivity Categorization in Alaska

A comprehensive understanding of atmospheric corrosion allows for accurate estimation of corrosion over time, promoting informed design choices and the implementation of effective anti-corrosion protection strategies. Despite advancements, there exists a knowledge gap in the corrosivity of metals in colder regions, such as Alaska, where the complexity of corrosion increases, causing the evaluation of atmospheric corrosion to be difficult. Multi-angle corrosion test racks were deployed at four test sites across Alaska, distinct in their environment near some of Alaska’s significant geographical locations, each equipped with weather sensors and chloride candles to evaluate atmospheric and environmental severity. Weather parameters recorded in this study include the air temperature, relative humidity, time of wetness at 0°, 30°, and 45° exposure angles, total rainfall, and aerosol chloride and sulfate deposition. The metal alloys studied include 6061-T6 Aluminum Alloy (UNS A96061), 2024-T3 Aluminum Alloy (UNS A92024), 304 Stainless Steel (UNS S30400), 1008 Carbon Steel (UNS G10080), and 110 Copper (UNS C11000). Coupon mass loss was measured, and corrosion rates were analyzed as a function of exposure angle. The influence of weather parameters on corrosion rates was then investigated. Based on experimental results, an equation was developed to more accurately model and estimate the corrosion rates and evaluate the atmospheric corrosivity in Alaska. This model quantifies experimental corrosion rates as a function of air temperature, relative humidity, sulfate deposition, chloride deposition, percent time wet, and exposure duration. Additional metal exposures were conducted at test sites in Hawaii and Florida to further investigate the influence of exposure angle on corrosion rate under assumed extreme atmospheric and environmental conditions.
